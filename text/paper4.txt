A B S T R A C T  
The Uterine Electromyogram often referred as the Electrohysterogram (EHG) is a signal that has the potential to 
be used for pregnancy monitoring and preterm risk evaluation, amongst other applications. Classification al­
gorithms success heavily rely on the adequacy of the selected features. In this study, Linear Mixed Models are 
used to evaluate a selected set of EHG features dependency on the gestational age, placental position, age, body 
mass index (BMI), gravidity and previous cesarean section. The obtained results point out, for example, to a 
dependency of some EHG features on the BMI and placental position, on the form of a low pass filtering effect. On 
the other hand, it was found that all the studied entropy features variation with gestational age was not sta­
tistically validated. Several conclusions can be drawn from the obtained results, which could be of interest for the 
increasing number of researchers using the EHG in machine learning computational algorithms, where the 
features are often selected without a disclosed criterion.   
1. Introduction 
Uterine Electromyography is a non-invasive technique for the 
recording of uterine contractile activity. The obtained signal is also 
called Electrohysterogram (EHG). The EHG, recorded through elec­
trodes placed on the abdominal surface, represents the bioelectrical 
signal resulting from the wave propagation of electrical potentials in the 
myometrium [1,2]. This new technique has been presented as an 
alternative tool to the widely used Tocography [3]. Tocogram limita­
tions include [3–8]:  
• Significant dependency on the maternal abdominal wall thickness;  
• Significant dependency on the transducer position relatively to the 
uterus;  
• Single channel mechanical sensor;  
• Signal loss following maternal movements;  
• Degraded sensitivity and specificity, namely for lower gestational 
ages;  
• Often used only as a contraction event rate counter. 
The gold standard for uterine contraction monitoring is the Intra­
uterine Pressure Catheter (IUPC) [9,10]. This is an invasive technique 
with associated health risks, which requires amniotic membrane 
rupture, thus being only applied in selected active labor contexts 
[11,12]. It is therefore not the selected technique for general pregnancy 
monitoring. 
The EHG is considered the most accurate and robust technique to 
record uterine contractile activity when compared to the TOCO and the 
IUPC [13,3]. In fact, the EHG has been reported as a validated alterna­
tive technique for pregnancy monitoring due to its non-invasive nature 
[14,15], multichannel capability [16], superior sensitivity and speci­
ficity and low dependency on the subject Body Mass Index (BMI) 
[17,18]. Marque et al. (1986) [19] used EHG signal processing in the 
time and spectral domains to investigate pregnancy and parturition. A 
monitoring device was proposed for contraction rate and efficiency 
evaluation.

Several features of the EHG signal can be extracted for its charac­
terization and analysis [20–22]. These features can be nonlinear or 
linear and can be obtained from either the time or the spectral domain. 
One of the main EHG application goals is preterm prediction, for 
which good results have been achieved [23–25]. Other authors use the 
EHG features to analyze the relationship between uterine contractions 
during labor and pregnancy [19,26,27] finding significant differences. 
Clearly, these methodologies heavily rely on the quality of the extracted 
features which, in turn, depend on the anthropometric and pregnancy 
variables such as gestational age. For instance, the contractions’ energy 
dependency on the gestational age, using a cohort of subjects must 
consider anthropometric and pre-gnancy variables including the BMI 
and the Placental Position. 
There are few studies dedicated to the analysis of the EHG features 
dependency on anthropometric and pregnancy variables. Caldeyro- 
Barcia (1959) [28] explored the intensity of uterine contractions 
throughout pregnancy (during the first 30 weeks) using the amniotic 
fluid pressure, recorded invasively. The presented results were 
measured with and without oxytocin administration and showed an 
evolution of uterine activity through gestational age, in all cases. In this 
study it is suggested a steady increase in uterine activity towards term 
pregnancy. Zahn (1984) [29] evaluated the occurrence rate of con­
tractions during pregnancy and found relevant peaks at 31st and 32nd 
gestational age. De Oliveira (2016) et al. [30] assessed the occurrence 
rate of contractions, according to progesterone administration, to reduce 
preterm risk in twin pregnancies, taking into account the progression 
throughout gestational weeks. In the placebo group, a reduction of the 
contractile occurrence was observed between 32nd and 34th gestational 
weeks. Moore (1994) [31] and Main et al. (1991) [32] observed an in­
crease in uterine contraction occurrence rate through pregnancy in 
TOCO recordings. Dickinson et al. (1997) [33] reported an increase in 
uterine activity, duration and amplitude of contractions throughout 
pregnancy (20–40 gestational weeks), using linear mixed models in a 
longitudinal study. The signal was recorded using the TOCO [33]. Ter­
rien et al. (2010) [34] investigated contraction duration evolution 
throughout pregnancy in a study regarding standard procedures for EHG 
storage and visualization. The results indicated weekly changes in 
contraction duration. Table 1 represents a summary of the state of the 
art regarding contractile activity dependency on selected features. 
BMI [17,18] and Placental Position [35–37] have been studied to 
investigate how they could affect both the EHG and the TOCO signals. 
Martinez et al. (2020) [38] studied the postpartum uterine dynamics 
after vaginal and elective cesarean. The EHG linear and nonlinear pa­
rameters were used for postpartum hemorrhage prediction. In other 
study, Cabo et al. (2020) [39] investigated the robustness of different 
EHG parameters obtained for a variety of obstetric scenarios. It was 
shown that the percentiles 90th and 10th outperformed median values 
in discriminating between obstetric scenarios. In the same study other 
trends were investigated such as the time reversibility which was not 
found a reliable indicator for EHG characterization.

The aim of this work is to evaluate the effect of anthropometric and 
pregnancy factors on important EHG extracted features. These features 
were selected due to their general use in machine learning and other 
artificial intelligence sub-fields [21]. In these scenarios feature selection 
is a crucial first step. Often the selection criteria is not always clear. 
Admittedly, a previous knowledge of the features’ characterristics, as far 
as the involved independent variables are concerned, would assist in the 
selection criteria. It is herein considered the features to be the output 
variable of multiple linear regression processes. This is thus a multi­
variable analysis. To the best of the authors knowledge three other 
studies were published, in the EHG and pregnancy context, using 
multivariable analysis: Dickinson et al. [33] (gestational age and 
maternal parity), Maine et al. (gestational age, maternal weight, age and 
parity) [32] and Moore [31] (gestational weeks and hour of the day). In 
the herein presented work, the following independent variables will be 
considered: Weeks before delivery (WBD), BMI, Placental Position, Age, 
Gravidity and Previous Cesarean. These variables were selected because 
they are available in the used database notation section. 
2. Materials and methods 
The EHG signals used in the present work are provided by the Ice­
landic 16-electrode EHG database [40]. This database comprises 122 
recordings from 45 pregnant women from the third trimester onwards. 
Thirty-two subjects visited the recording center between 1 and 7 times. 
Cesarean delivery cases were excluded from the study to avoid bias due 
to calculation of the WBD parameter. Labor cases were also excluded to 
avoid non-representative small values of the WBD. The remaining of the 
33 subjects were initially eligible corresponding to 5105 contractions. 
This dataset can be considered longitudinal since there are multiple 
recordings per women, although each woman visited the center in 
different gestational ages, and with a different number of total visits. 
The maternal anthropometric characterristics and other relevant infor­
mation of the pregnant women enrolled in this study are described in 
Table 2. 
The EHG signal sample rate was 200 Hz and an anti-aliasing filter 
with a low cut-off frequency of 100 Hz was used. The recordings took 
place from 2008 to 2010 and the data was published as open source in 
2015 on physionet.org. 
The data was recorded using 16 electrodes in a 4-by-4 grid. In the 
present study, a bipolar channel was used, to reduce the common mode 
noise components such as maternal respiration movements and power 
supply interference. The selection arrangement for this study was the 
bipolar channel 4–13, as shown in Fig. 1, and explained in [41,42]. 
The data was processed with MATLAB® and R programing 
languages. 
2.1. Signal pre-processing and contraction detection 
The description of the adopted signal filtering and the automatic 
contraction detection can be found in [41]. 
Pre-processing included sample rate reduction from 200 Hz to 4 Hz 
and passband wavelet filtering between 0.1 Hz and 1 Hz [43].

requires the dependent variable to be normally distributed. Therefore, 
normality was assessed by distribution plots (Fig. 2) and data skewness 
was investigated. For each feature in which normality assumption was 
clearly not met, a log transformation was applied.

Model fixed-effects validation was based on the backward stepwise 
procedure by constructing an ANOVA table to each model. The fixed- 
effect parameter with the highest p-value was removed and a new 
model is constructed. If this new model was statistically significant (F 
test) from the previous, then the simplified model was considered to next 
iteration. This procedure is repeated until the final model was selected 
[59]. 
The following R programming language packages were used: lme4 
[60], lmerTest [61] and MuMIn. This last package was used to compute 
the conditional R2 presented in the results, that accounts for the ratio of 
the explained variability, taking into account the linear mixed models 
structure [62].

parameter is 0.17 for the Fuzzy Entropy model, while the highest 0.6 is 
for the model explaining the log Peak Amplitude. This implies that the 
model can explain 60% of the total variation and it is also the obtained 
best fit in the results.

The Median Frequency was found in this model to be dependent on

Log Energy has been found increasing as labor approaches 
(decreasing WBD values) with P% = −55%. This result is in agreement 
with the general consensus [28,30,63]. Additionally, this feature does 
not show dependency on the anthropo-metric variables. This fact cor­
roborates the EHG as being a robust tool for pregnancy monitoring. 
4.4. Log Shannon Entropy 
Log Shannon Entropy has been found increasing as labor approaches. 
Additionally, this feature does not show dependency on the anthropo­
metric variables. It stands out that the model parameters for this feature 
are identical to the Log Energy ones. Also, the P% are identical for both 
features.

WBD and BMI were found as significant to describe the Log Peak 
Amplitude feature. As labor approaches, the log peak amplitude in­

the one reported for uterine contractions by Main et al. [32], where this 
parameter has been found to significantly increase with gestational age. 
Different results were obtained by Hemthanon et al. [47] where the 
average EHG amplitude was not found to be correlated with gestational 
age. Other comments regarding these features are presented in the 
Conclusion section.

None of the independent variables had a detectable effect on this 
feature. Therefore, the feature is represented in Table 3 but not in

However these two findings are not in accordance with Dickinson et al. 
[33] for which contraction duration generally increased 0.7 s per 
gestational week. Additionally there is also agreement with the results 
presented by Terrien et al. [34] where no significant effect of WBD was

The above explained features without comparative literature results 
indicate the non-existence of published equivalent results, as far as the 
authors of this work are aware of. 
It stands out that Fuzzy Entropy, Permutation Entropy, Conditional 
Entropy and Lyapunov Exponent are only explained by the Placental 
Position according to the used model. The variations ΔY are −81.390e- 
3, −17.714e-03, −40.030e-3, 76.840e-3, respectively. This suggests that 
these features should therefore be used with caution in applications for 
pregnancy monitoring where the WBD or the herein studied anthropo­
metric variables are used. 
Upon on observation of Table 3 it was concluded that features rep­
resented by two independent variables do not exhibit random effects 
dependency, except for the Log Peak Amplitude. This could demonstrate 
that features explained by two independent variables have their random 
effects depen-dency explained by the latter. 
For the following features: Log Duration and Correlation Dimension 
no variable was found to have a significant effect at the selected p-value 
cut-off to explain the behavior of the feature variation with the inde­
pendent variables. 
5. Conclusion 
In recent times, machine learning applications using the EHG have 
caught the attention in the pregnancy monitoring and preterm risk 
evaluation research community. Often, EHG based features are used in 
these works without the feature selection criteria being disclosed. 
However, the nature of the selected features will certainly impact the 
classification algorithms’ results. This work evaluated the effect of 
anthropometric and pregnancy factors on popular EHG extracted fea­
tures. The obtained results could help in selecting appropriate EHG 
features for classification tasks. The herein presented work, along with 
other three reports [31–33] are the only material published in this 
subject, to the best of the authors’ knowledge. As shown in the results 
section, a significant number of the obtained outcomes agree with pre­
viously published results, however for other features some disagreement 
was detected. This is expected since the used methodologies and datasets 
are not the same. More work should be done in this field using common 
parametrization, methodologies and data. 
In studies of this nature, the used dataset structure is an important 
factor. Since the longitudinal data structure is irregular, estimation bias 
might exist, especially considering the cases where WBD was found as a 
significant variable due to its heterogenous sampling distribution. 
Considering a model structure without the random effects, there is 
indubitably induced bias by having multiple observations per women 
and each woman having a different contribution to the estimates, due to 
the different number of recording sessions. As mentioned before, there 
are only 3 cases of previous cesarean delivery compared with 72 cases of 
non-cesarean recordings, which makes it an unbalanced design. None­
theless, this variable was included so that it could be considered as a 
reference for further studies using databases that could have a more 
suitable data balancing for this particular case. The used database, 
likewise the other popular EHG datasets, lacks homogeneous sampling 
in parameters such as the WBD. Also, the number of recording sessions 
per woman is not the same. In addition, not all recording sessions occur 
at the same gestation age for all women. It is often not possible to 
schedule recording sessions to get statistical homogeneously distributed 
data. The number of recording sessions per woman would ideally be 
higher than the average portraited in the used database and with a 
common value. 
This is hardly achievable, as the enrollment in the recording sessions 
is voluntary and it has been often observed reluctance to adhere to a 
steady recording protocol, for a variety of reasons. Researchers are left 
with unbalanced data which requires techniques whose complexity may 
lead to an extra effort in the results interpretation and validation. 
Despite the above enunciated predicaments, regarding EHG database 
acquisition paradigms, it was possible to statistically validate the used 
models. A set of selected features currently used in EHG classification 
algorithms has been accessed using Linear Regression methods 
regarding their variation with WBD and anthropometric variables. 
Interesting conclusions have been found which are presented in the 
Results section and Table 4. Placental Position has been found to impact 
all the entropy studied features, except for the Shannon Entropy. For 
Non-Anterior Placenta cases, all the affected entropy features display a 
value reduction. This indicates that anterior placental implantation in­
duces an increase on the information content or uncertainty in the EHG 
contraction signal. Further electrophysiological studies are required for 
a complete understanding of this property. This finding should be 
examined along with the Non-Anterior Placental impact on the spectral 
features to be mentioned below. The Lyapunov Exponent, which is a 
measure of chaos, increases for the Non-Anterior Placenta cases. This 
feature is a measure of signal dependency on the initial conditions of the 
system. Again, this requires further investigation. 
Both the Log Mean Frequency and the Log Median Frequency show a 
reduction (P% = −11%) with increasing BMI values, thus implying a 
low-pass filter effect mainly due to the fat layer between the myome­
trium and the acquisition electrodes. For mono-component signals this 
may imply a bandwidth reduction. The Log Mean Frequency shows a 
reduction (P% = −7%) for Non-Anterior Placenta cases. This interesting 
result can be interpreted under the knowledge that placental implan­
tation on the myometrium impacts the later electrical properties [37]. It 
is apparent that the placenta implantation in the myometrium incurs in 
the increase of the higher frequency components of the EHG contrac­
tions. The electrophysiological mechanisms behind this effect remain to 
be investigated. 
The Log Energy feature has been found to decrease with the WBD 
(increasing with the gestational age) with P% = −55%. This indicates 
that uterus maturation implies higher contractions’ energy. An identical 
result has been found in the Log Peak Amplitude as far as the WBD is

concerned, although this feature also being explained by the BMI, unlike 
the Log Energy. This can be interpreted as the contractions’ peaks 
having low energy content despite their amplitude (short lived events). 
On the other hand, the low-pass filtering effect of the BMI makes this 
variable to impact the Log Peak Amplitude P% = −50%. 
The EHG independence on the BMI has been presented as a flagship 
of the method. In this study, it has been found that the three of the eleven 
studied features depending on the BMI are: Log Mean Frequency, Log 
Median Frequency and the Log Peak Amplitude. As stated above, the Log 
Peak Amplitude dependency on the BMI is due to its low-pass filter ef­
fect, for higher BMI values, on the signal low energy sharp peaks. One 
might therefore conclude that the BMI impacts mostly spectral related 
features. The used dataset (Icelandic Database) does not include preterm 
cases. Therefore, feature investigation for preterm cases was not inves­
tigated in this study. 
This work is a contribution for EHG feature selection for classifica­
tion algorithms applied to pregnancy monitoring and preterm risk 
evaluation. A complete electrophysiological interpretation of the ob­
tained results is a task beyond the scope of this work and is probably an 
assignment for which it is expected a greater involvement in multidis­
ciplinary cooperation. It is, however, in the authors’ future research 
planning, as well as using the presented methodology in other EHG 
databases.
