A B S T R A C T
Electrohysterography (EHG) represents a promising computational approach for non-invasive monitoring of 
uterine activity during pregnancy and labor. This review summarizes the advancements in signal processing 
techniques and machine learning algorithms that have been applied to enhance the utility of EHG. Key topics 
include the extraction and analysis of uterine electrical signals, classification of contractions, and prediction 
of obstetric outcomes such as preterm and labor/non-labor states. The review emphasizes computational 
methodologies for signal processing and extraction, including empirical mode decomposition or wavelet 
transform, and for data classification, such as neural networks or support vector machine, highlighting their 
performance and limitations. Despite significant progress, challenges persist, such as the lack of standardized 
protocols, limited datasets, and inconsistent evaluation and annotation metrics, which hinder broader clinical 
adoption. The integration of additional clinical markers, simultaneous monitoring of maternal and fetal health, 
and the development of wearable systems for telemedicine present exciting opportunities for future research.

1. Introduction
Effective uterine contractions are an essential part of physiological 
labor, ensuring the safe arrival of the newborn. These rhythmic con-
tractions of the uterine muscles are crucial for cervical dilation and the 
subsequent expulsion of the fetus. During labor, contractions not only 
help move the baby down the birth canal but also ensure its correct 
positioning and facilitate its progress out of the mother’s body [1]. 
Without effective uterine contractions, the birth process cannot proceed 
naturally, often necessitating medical interventions such as a cesarean 
section [2].
The uterine contractions can be assessed using manual palpation. 
However, its accuracy heavily depends on the experience and sub-
jective judgment of the physician [3]. For this reason, the electronic 
measurement techniques are commonly used in clinical practice. The 
most widely used method is external tocography, which measures 
pressure changes via a tocodynamometer (TOCO) secured to the preg-
nant woman’s abdomen with a fixation belt. While simple to use, 
non-invasive and passive, this method is significantly affected by ma-
ternal movements and high body mass index (BMI). Additionally, its 
accuracy relies on proper sensor placement and optimal belt tight-
ness, which can restrict the mother’s mobility and hinder continu-
ous monitoring [4]. Another available method involves invasive mea-
surement of intrauterine pressure using a pressure catheter (IUPC), 
which is inserted transvaginally during labor after rupture of the fetal 
membranes. This approach carries risks such as intrauterine infection, 
uterine perforation, or placental abruption [5].
The limitations of these conventional methods, coupled with ad-
vancements in technology, have shifted scientific attention toward 
electrohysterography (EHG). This technique evaluates uterine muscle 
activity by detecting electrical signals generated during contractions. 
The first EHG recording, achieved by Bode in 1931 [6], used two 
electric needles placed on the abdominal surface connected to a gal-
vanometer. Later, in 1950, Steer and Hertsch [7] recorded uterine 
electrical activity using six channels linked to electrodes placed on 
the abdomen of a pregnant woman. Since then, research in EHG has 
significantly progressed, driven by modern technologies and advanced 
signal processing algorithms.
Today, high-quality EHG recordings can be obtained non-invasively 
from the 19th week of pregnancy using surface electrodes placed 
on the abdominal wall. This method provides detailed information 
about the frequency, intensity, duration, and coordination of uter-
ine contractions [8]. Compared to external TOCO, EHG is a motion-
resistant, BMI-independent approach suitable for continuous monitor-
ing [4,9]. A study [8] reported significantly higher sensitivity for 
detecting uterine contractions using EHG (86.0%–98.0%) compared 
to TOCO (46.0%–73.6%). Obstetricians also found EHG more inter-
pretable than TOCO traces. Furthermore, the use of surface electrodes 
is comfortable for pregnant women and does not restrict mobility. 
According to another study [9], 80.8% of women preferred EHG, 17.3% 
preferred IUPC, and only 1.9% were satisfied with external TOCO.
In addition to uterine activity, abdominally placed electrodes for 
EHG can simultaneously record maternal and fetal heart activity using

the non-invasive fetal electrocardiography (NI-fECG) technique. Stud-
ies [10,11] have demonstrated that NI-fECG can extract both fetal 
(fECG) and maternal (mECG) signals from the mother’s abdominal sur-
face (these abdominally recorded signals are referred to as abdominal 
ECG (aECG)), enabling accurate fetal heart rate (fHR) and maternal

A recent study [12] found that simultaneous NI-fECG and EHG 
monitoring was more accurate and reliable than conventional car-

These methods were less affected by maternal obesity, and 90% of 
women did not require a switch to conventional monitoring. Addition-
ally, simultaneous monitoring of fHR and uterine contractions has been 
shown to improve the detection of fetal distress [13].
Despite its potential, the EHG technique faces several challenges 
that hinder its widespread clinical adoption. Extracting a high-quality 
EHG signal is inherently difficult due to its low frequency (0.01 Hz–
5 Hz) and very low amplitude (50 μV–5 mV), which make it highly 
susceptible to artifacts such as maternal and fetal movements, respi-
ration, and electrical cardiac activity [14]. These issues are further 
compounded by the lack of standardization in key aspects of the 
method. There are no universally accepted guidelines for electrode 
placement, signal recording techniques, or definitions of frequency 
ranges for EHG signals, with considerable variability reported in the 
literature [4]. Moreover, the characteristics of EHG signals evolve 
throughout pregnancy as uterine muscle tissue develops, leading to 
changes in both time and frequency domain properties [15].
To address these challenges, advanced signal processing methods 
are needed to filter noise and ensure reliable data extraction. Once 
these limitations are resolved, the EHG technique, especially when 
combined with NI-fECG, could become a new standard of antenatal 
care. It holds promise for continuous and non-invasive monitoring of 
uterine contractions and fetal heart activity, even enabling remote 
telemonitoring for e-health applications [14].
Several reviews have been published [8,16–22] that summarize the 
current knowledge in the field of EHG:
• Devedeux et al. [21] focused on the analysis of uterine elec-
trical activity during different stages of pregnancy and labor, 
with particular emphasis on its frequency characteristics and 
propagation patterns within the myometrial tissue. Similarly, 
Vinken et al. [16] investigated frequency parameters of EHG sig-
nals for distinguishing premature contractions leading to preterm 
labor but did not extensively cover signal processing or classifi-
cation methods.
• Vlemminx et al. [8] and Huber et al. [20] summarized the clinical 
benefits of EHG compared to conventional techniques but did 
not provide a comprehensive analysis of algorithms, datasets, or 
evaluation metrics.
• Jossou et al. [17] examined electrode placement, number, and 
type but did not provide an in-depth analysis of EHG signal 
characteristics, interference, datasets, or signal processing and 
classification algorithms.
• Casado et al. [18] and Xu et al. [19] focused on preterm la-
bor diagnosis. Casado et al. [18] explored temporal, spectral, 
nonlinear, and propagation analyses, including artificial intelli-
gence (AI) and machine learning (ML) classification but lacked 
broad summaries of datasets, evaluation metrics, and extraction 
methods. Xu et al. [19] reviewed datasets, feature extraction, 
segmentation, and classification but omitted detailed discussion 
of evaluation metrics, electrode deployment, public datasets, and 
signal extraction algorithms.
• Liu et al. [22] provided a comprehensive overview of wearable 
sensors and AI applications in pregnancy monitoring, covering 
modalities such as EHG, fHR, and maternal physical activity. 
However, the review adopted a broad perspective, emphasizing 
hardware design, data transmission, and energy efficiency in 
telemonitoring systems, while lacking a detailed analysis focused 
on EHG-specific characteristics, signal processing pipelines, fea-
ture extraction techniques, and the comparative performance of 
classification algorithms.
Unlike previous reviews that addressed only specific aspects of 
EHG—such as physiology without a detailed analysis of algorithms [21], 
frequency-based indicators related to preterm birth [16], clinical com-
parisons with TOCO/IUPC without coverage of data or metrics [8,
20], electrode configurations without signal analysis [17], wearable 
sensors and telemonitoring systems [22], or narrowly focused AI/ML 
approaches lacking a systematic overview of datasets, metrics, and 
feature extraction steps [18,19]—the present work integrates both 
clinical and technical perspectives and provides a comprehensive map-
ping of the field. Specifically, it covers the nature of EHG signals and 
noise sources, electrode layouts and commercial systems, available 
public datasets and their characteristics, evaluation standards, and 
the entire processing pipeline—from preprocessing and signal extrac-
tion through contraction detection to classification (term/preterm; la-
bor/non-labor)—with a particular emphasis on modern ML/AI method-
ologies. The present review covers the most recent literature from 2010 
to 2025 and, in parallel, identify and discuss research gaps from both 
technical (signal, feature extraction, modeling, metrics, data) and clini-
cal (feasibility, interpretation, workflow integration, multimodal signal 
fusion) perspectives, and offer concrete recommendations for future 
research directions. The scope of this review compared to previous 
works is summarized in Table 1.
Google Scholar, PubMed, and Scopus were used to identify rele-
vant sources for this review. The search employed combinations of 
key terms related to EHG: ‘‘Electrohysterography’’, ‘‘Uterine electromyo-
graphy’’, and ‘‘Uterine contractions’’, combined with signal processing 
and analysis terms: ‘‘Extraction’’, ‘‘Processing’’, ‘‘Analysis’’, ‘‘Detection’’, 
and ‘‘Classification’’ forming 15 unique queries. Low-quality conference 
papers and articles published before 2010 were excluded. A total of 46 
studies that tested advanced signal processing methods or AI/ML-based 
algorithms met these criteria and form the foundation of this review.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 
discusses the characteristics of EHG signals, focusing on different types 
of uterine contractions and common sources of interference. Section 3 
examines EHG signal recording, emphasizing the number and place-
ment of sensing electrodes, and provides a summary of publicly avail-
able EHG datasets and their parameters. Section 4 reviews advanced 
algorithms, including AI/ML-based approaches, for processing and an-
alyzing EHG signals. This section is divided into five thematic areas: 
EHG preprocessing, signal extraction, uterine contraction detection, 
term/preterm birth prediction, and labor/non-labor record classifi-
cation. Evaluation metrics used to assess extraction, detection, and 
classification quality are also discussed.  Section 5 summarizes the prin-
cipal findings of this review, while Section 6 discusses these findings 
in the context of existing research. Key directions for future investiga-
tion to further advance the field are presented in Section 7. Finally, 
Section 8 concludes the review and its contributions.
2. EHG signal characteristics
EHG signals arise from the electrical activity of the myometrial cells, 
which generate action potentials initiated by changes in cell membrane 
potential. The coordination of these impulses leads to uterine contrac-
tions. The transmission of electrical potentials across myometrial cell 
membranes is facilitated by hormone-dependent development of gap 
junctions, which form low-resistance electrical pathways within the 
myometrium [3].
The frequency of action potential discharges, the number of myome-
trial cells activated simultaneously, and the duration of each discharge 
determine the frequency, amplitude, and duration of uterine contrac-
tions [20]. The force generated by the uterus depends on the propaga-
tion extent of action potentials, reflected in the spectral characteristics

The amplitude of EHG signals ranges from 50 μV–5 mV [14]. 
However, defined frequency ranges for EHG signals vary across the 
literature [4,23]. EHG signals consist of slow waves, resulting from 
abdominal skin stretching, and fast waves, associated with uterine 
muscle activity [23] and are defined as follows:
• Slow waves – According to Devedeux et al. [21], the frequency 
range of slow waves is 0.014–0.033 Hz, while Jezewski et al. [24] 
identified a broader range of 0.03–0.1 Hz.
• Fast waves – Fast waves are further categorized into low-frequency 
and high-frequency bands. Low-frequency waves occur during 
pregnancy and labor, while high-frequency waves are associated 
with effective labor contractions [23].
– Low-Frequency Waves: Vrhovec et al. [25] reported a fre-
quency range of 0.1–1.2 Hz, whereas Khalil et al. [26] 
defined a narrower range of 0.2–0.45 Hz.
– High-Frequency Waves: The range for high-frequency waves 
was determined by Khalil et al. [26] as 0.8–3 Hz, and by 
Vrhovec et al. [25] as 1.2–4.7 Hz.
2.1. Uterine contraction classification
Uterine contractions are a key indicator of labor onset and pro-
gression, though they occur throughout pregnancy and postpartum. 
During pregnancy and labor, contractions vary in frequency, duration, 
and intensity [27]. In early pregnancy, uterine electrical activity is low 
and uncoordinated, gradually becoming more intense, frequent, and 
synchronous as gestation progresses (see illustration in Fig. 1). While 
mild uterine activity is common throughout pregnancy, painful contrac-
tions before 37 weeks may indicate threatened preterm labor [20]. The 
primary types of uterine contractions, summarized in Table 2, include:
• Alvarez waves – Alvarez waves, defined by Alvarez et al. [28], 
are local, asynchronous contractions occurring randomly in the 
uterus during the first 30 weeks of pregnancy. These contractions 
are characterized by high frequency (1–2 per minute) and low 
intensity [27,29], with a frequency range of 0.2–1 Hz [30]. 
Recent research suggests Alvarez waves may precede more syn-
chronized contractions, potentially leading to preterm labor [23], 
though this link remains debated, with some studies reporting low 
predictive value [29].
• Braxton-Hicks – Braxton-Hicks contractions [31] are irregular, 
localized contractions starting around the 20th week of preg-
nancy. These ‘‘false labor’’ contractions occur infrequently (1 
every 3–4 h) [32] and do not cause cervical dilation. They do not 
increase in frequency, duration, or intensity, and may disappear 
and reappear. After the 36th week, these contractions may inten-
sify, but they still do not signal true labor [23]. Their frequency 
range is not well defined in the literature.
• Early labor contractions — This type of contractions occurs after 
the 36th week but before active labor [23]. These contractions are 
less intense and regular than late labor contractions, occurring at 
a lower frequency (1 every 10 min) and lasting approximately

Although specific frequency ranges are not well documented,

• Late labor contractions – Late labor contractions are intense and 
highly synchronized, occurring close to delivery [23]. These 
contractions are more regular, with higher frequency (3 every

upper uterus and progress in a coordinated manner throughout, 
achieving simultaneous peak contraction across the uterus. Their 
primary role is full cervical dilation and fetal expulsion [27].
• Long duration low frequency waves (LDBF) — LDBF waves are char-
acterized by long duration (several minutes) and low frequency 
(0–1 Hz) [32,33]. These waves, common in late pregnancy, are 
associated with uterine hypertonicity, where the uterus remains 
contracted without complete relaxation. This can cause uterine 
hypoxia and fetal stress due to compromised blood flow.
• Other contractions — Less well-described contractions include Le-
man waves, which are associated with low amplitude [32] and 
low frequency [23]. Contractions may also occur postpartum, 
though they are not yet thoroughly characterized in the literature.
2.2. Noise definition
In addition to uterine contractions, which constitute the useful 
EHG signal, various interfering electrical signals and artifacts are also 
detected from the surface of the pregnant woman’s abdomen. These 
overlapping signals can obscure or distort the EHG and must be ad-
dressed to ensure accurate signal interpretation. The most significant 
interfering signals and artifacts include:
• Maternal ECG – Representing maternal cardiac activity, the mECG 
signal is a prominent component present in all recorded chan-
nels [33,34]. Its frequency range is 0.5–100 Hz [35].
• Fetal ECG – The fECG signal represents fetal cardiac activity 
and typically has a very low amplitude, often difficult to record 
alongside the EHG [33,36]. Its frequency range is similar to that 
of the mECG [35].
• Maternal and fetal respiration – Respiratory artifacts, especially 
those originating from the mother, are a significant source of 
interference. While they do not show large amplitude varia-
tions, they are highly disruptive because they overlap with the 
dominant frequency band of the EHG (0.1–1 Hz) [23,33].
• Electromyography noise – This interference originates from con-
tractions of the abdominal muscles. These signals have low ampli-
tude and minimal impact on the EHG, with a frequency content 
around 30 Hz [23,33].
• Motion artefacts – Movement of the mother or fetus generates 
motion artifacts, which vary significantly based on the type of 
movement. The frequency range of these artifacts spans from 1 Hz 
to hundreds of Hz [14,33].
• Baseline wander – Baseline wander disrupts the stability and uni-
formity of the isoelectric line in the EHG signal. This artifact often 
results from maternal movement or poor electrode–skin contact 
and has frequency content up to 1 Hz [14].
• External electromagnetic noise – This interference arises from elec-
tromagnetic fields generated by electrical devices and power 
supply networks. It typically does not significantly affect the EHG 
signal, as its frequency range is concentrated around 50 or 60 Hz 
and their harmonics [33].

3. Acquisition of EHG signals
EHG signals are recorded non-invasively using electrodes placed on 
the surface of the pregnant woman’s abdomen. Classical wet Ag/AgCl 
electrodes are predominantly used for this purpose. The application of 
these electrodes varies in several aspects, including their type (monopo-
lar, bipolar, concentric, or grid), number, and placement, as detailed 
in [17] and illustrated in Fig. 2.
3.1. Electrodes placement
The proper placement of sensing electrodes on a pregnant woman’s 
abdomen is critical to effectively capturing the electrical activity gen-
erated by uterine muscles during contractions. To minimize skin impe-
dance and enhance signal quality, it is recommended to prepare the 
skin with an abrasive gel prior to electrode placement [37–42].
A significant limitation of the EHG technique is the lack of stan-
dardization. Currently, no uniform guidelines exist for electrode place-
ment or the methodology for recording EHG signals [17]. This subsec-
tion summarizes the most commonly used placements of sensing and 
reference/ground electrodes based on the available literature.
3.1.1. Sensing electrodes
The number of sensing electrodes varies widely, from as few as 
2 [41,43,44] to as many as 64 [45]. For basic EHG signal char-
acterization, 2–4 electrodes are sufficient [46]. However, for more 
detailed signal analysis, larger setups with 11, 16, or 64 electrodes are 
preferred [17].
Electrodes are typically placed around the navel, either along the 
vertical [24,47] or horizontal [38,41] axis of the uterus. Some re-
searchers have placed electrodes along two vertical lines around the 
navel [48,49], while others have used both vertical and horizontal 
configurations [39,40,50–53]. The inter-electrode distance varies con-
siderably, with reported values of 3–4 cm [54], 5 cm [24,47,55], 
7 cm [42,50], and 8 cm [38].
When a larger number of electrodes is used, such as in a matrix 
grid [56–59], the grid typically covers a significant portion of the uter-
ine surface, allowing detailed mapping of electrical activity. Effective 
inter-electrode distances for grids are reported as 17.5 mm [58,59] or 
25 mm [44].
3.1.2. Reference and ground electrodes
Various configurations exist for the placement of reference and 
ground electrodes. When a single reference electrode is used without a 
ground electrode, it is typically placed on the left [24], right [40] side 
of the subject, or the right thigh [42]. For two reference electrodes, 
they are usually placed on both hips [56,57] or thighs [44].
For setups with only a ground electrode, it is placed on the right [43,
55] or left hip [51]. When both reference and ground electrodes are 
used, the reference electrode is often positioned below the navel and 
the ground electrode on the right side [37], or one on each side of 
the subject [38,54,58]. Another promising configuration places both 
electrodes next to each other in the middle of the abdomen, with the 
reference electrode just above the navel [39].
3.2. Available measurement systems
The previous subsections described a general overview of EHG ac-
quisition using standard bio-potential measurement systems, including 
electrode placement options and different recording configurations. 
In contrast, this subsection focuses on systems—both commercial de-
vices and research prototypes—designed specifically for EHG monitor-
ing, typically with fixed hardware configurations, electrode layouts, 
and acquisition settings. Several commercially available and research-
developed systems [5,60–65] are in use for EHG acquisition. These 
platforms differ in channel count, electrode arrangement, signal reso-
lution, and intended clinical application, which directly influence their 
suitability for research datasets.
3.2.1. Commercial devices
Commercial devices such as Bloomlife (Bloomlife, USA) [60], Nemo 
Fetal Monitoring Patch (Nemo Healthcare, The Netherlands) [61], and 
Novii Wireless Patch System (GE Healthcare, USA; previously Monica 
Healthcare, UK) [62] provide wireless, adhesive-based solutions for 
uterine activity and fHR monitoring. In addition to these NI-fECG–
based devices, Nemo Healthcare also offers a commercial system ded-
icated solely to EHG-based contraction monitoring, the PUREtrace 
labour registration system [5]. These devices typically employ 3–5 
disposable Ag/AgCl electrodes, sample at rates of 250–500 Hz, and 
incorporate on-board preprocessing to transmit compressed data. Their 
advantages include patient comfort, minimal movement restriction, and 
validated reliability in routine obstetric use. Limitations for research 
purposes include proprietary filtering, restricted access to raw signals, 
and variability in documentation of electrode placement. 
Another example is the INVU system (Nuvo Group, Israel/USA) [63], 
which is intended for remote pregnancy monitoring and combines elec-
trical modalities (EHG and NI-fECG) with mechano-acoustic monitoring 
(fetal phonocardiography–fPCG). Unlike patch-based systems, INVU 
uses a reusable belt without disposable electrodes, reducing ongoing 
consumable costs and simplifying patient setup. Additional advantages 
include patient self-application, integration with telehealth platforms 
for remote review by clinicians, and continuous monitoring without re-
quiring in-clinic visits. These features make INVU particularly suitable 
for long-term home-based pregnancy surveillance.

groups to allow full control over hardware, signal processing, and 
electrode configurations. Examples include the FetalCare system [64], 
which integrates EHG and NI-fECG acquisition with empirical mode 
decomposition-based signal separation, and the Nemo Grid proto-
types [65], which employ high-density electrode arrays for mapping

3.3. Available datasets
To evaluate the effectiveness of extraction and classification algo-
rithms, it is essential to test them on real clinical data. Currently, 
only four publicly available databases with real EHG recordings exist 
and these will be summarized in this subsection. A summary of the 
characteristics of these publicly available datasets is presented in Table 
3.
3.3.1. Icelandic 16-electrode electrohysterogram database (Icelandic)
The Icelandic database [58] includes 122 EHG recordings from 45 
pregnant women during pregnancy or suspected labor. Among the 
recordings, 2 resulted in preterm birth, 84 in term birth, 20 in induced 
labor, and 16 in cesarean sections. Data were collected between 2008 
and 2010 at Landspitali University Hospital and Akureyri Hospital, 
focusing on normal singleton pregnancies without known risk factors 
for preterm birth. Each participant contributed 2–7 recordings.
Recordings were performed using 16 monopolar Ag/AgCl electrodes 
arranged in a 4 × 4 grid with a 17.5 mm inter-electrode distance. Ref-
erence and ground electrodes were placed over the iliac crests, though 
placement was not standardized. Signals were sampled at 200 Hz with 
16-bit resolution and a 100 Hz antialiasing filter. TOCO data were 
simultaneously recorded.
The average recording duration was 61 min during pregnancy 
(range: 19–86 min) and 36 min during labor (range: 8–64 min). Gesta-
tional ages ranged from 29 weeks and 5 days to 41 weeks and 5 days. 
The dataset includes TOCO recordings, annotations of clinical events 
(e.g., contractions, maternal position changes, and fetal movements), 
and clinical information.
3.3.2. Term–preterm electrohysterogram database (TPEHG)
The TPEHG database [50] contains 300 EHG recordings, each 
30 min long, from 300 different women collected between 1997 and 
2005 at the University Medical Centre Ljubljana. Of these, 262 record-
ings were from pregnancies ending in term labor (143 before 26 
weeks and 119 at or after 26 weeks), while 38 recordings were from 
pregnancies ending in preterm labor (19 before 26 weeks and 19 at or 
after 26 weeks).
Four AgCl2 electrodes were placed in two horizontal rows 3.5 cm 
above and below the umbilicus with a 7 cm inter-electrode distance. 
Signals were sampled at 20 Hz with 16-bit resolution and a ±2.5 mV 
amplitude range. Three bipolar signals were derived from the electrode 
configuration. The dataset also provides three filtered versions of each 
signal using Butterworth filters with cutoffs of 0.08–4 Hz, 0.3–3 Hz, 
and 0.3–4 Hz. Clinical information is included.
3.3.3. Term–preterm electrohysterogram dataset with tocogram (TPEHGT)
The TPEHGT dataset [66] consists of 26 recordings collected at the 
University Medical Centre Ljubljana, each 30 min long, from women 
between 26 and 36 weeks of gestation. These are divided into two 
groups: 13 recordings from pregnancies ending in preterm birth (av-
erage gestational age: 33.7 ±1.97 weeks) and 13 term pregnancies 
(average gestational age: 38.1± 1.04 weeks).

4. EHG signal processing and analysis
EHG signals contain clinically significant information about uterine 
activity; however, this information is often masked by various interfer-
ences due to the signals’ low frequency and amplitude. The presence 
of such interference can distort the signal’s power spectral density 
and hinder the accurate identification of uterine contractions [14]. 
Therefore, it is crucial to eliminate these interferences to obtain a 
clean, unaltered EHG signal, enabling effective analysis and accurate 
diagnostic insights.
This section covers the most important aspects in EHG signal pro-
cessing, analysis, and classification and is structured as follows. The 
techniques for preprocessing and extracting EHG signals are detailed 
in Section 4.2 and Section 4.3, respectively. After obtaining a high-
quality EHG signal, further analysis can be performed. Common areas 
of research include: Detection of uterine contractions (Section 4.4), 
Term/preterm birth prediction (Section 4.5), Labor/non-labor records 
classification (Section 4.6). Finally, to objectively evaluate the perfor-
mance of EHG signal processing and analysis methods, a range of eval-
uation metrics are used. These metrics are summarized in Section 4.1. 
The domains of EHG signal processing and analysis are illustrated in 
Fig. 3.

4.2. Preprocessing
Preprocessing is an essential step in the analysis of EHG signals. The 
primary objective of preprocessing is to remove unwanted interferences 
while preserving the useful signal that represents uterine activity. 
Common sources of interference include baseline wander, power line 
noise, maternal/fetal motion artifacts, and maternal/fetal respiratory 
activity, as discussed in Section 2.2.
In the field of EHG analysis, preprocessing typically involves the 
use of frequency-selective filters. A low-pass filter (LPF) with a cutoff 
frequency of 3 Hz has been widely employed [46,54,74–76]. Alter-
natively, band-pass filters (BPF) are used to retain specific frequency 
bands with varying bandwidths which are summarized in Table 4.
4.3. Extraction methods
Preprocessing alone may not be sufficient to eliminate all interfering 
signals, particularly maternal or fetal cardiac activity. To address this, 
some studies have employed additional filtering techniques such as the 
Wiener filter [83] or the median filter [46,54,74,75] to enhance the 
quality of EHG signals.
However, due to the overlap of mECG, fECG, and EHG signals 
in both the time and frequency domains, advanced signal processing 
methods are often necessary for their effective separation. This sub-
section provides an overview of studies that applied these advanced 
signal processing or ML-based methods, as summarized in Table 5. 
Additionally, since abdominal recordings can be used to extract fECG 
and mECG signals alongside EHG, a column has been included in the 
table to indicate whether this aspect was addressed by the respective 
authors.
4.3.1. Wavelet transform for signal extraction
The wavelet transform (WT) is an advanced signal processing
method that provides a time–frequency representation of the input 
signal. It enables the analysis of both slowly and rapidly changing signal 
components, making it particularly suitable for non-stationary signals. 
Proper functioning of the WT algorithm requires optimal parameter 
settings, including the wavelet type and the number of decomposition 
levels [84]. However, these settings may differ from case to case.
The effectiveness of WT in removing interference from EHG signals 
was demonstrated in a study by Limem et al. [85]. The study found that 
the quality of extraction was significantly influenced by the choice of 
the mother wavelet and the number of decomposition levels. Optimal 
results were achieved using the Daubechies 4 wavelet with three decom-
position levels, yielding an average SNR of 8.35 dB. A key advantage of 
WT is its ability to eliminate interference while preserving the essential 
characteristics of the useful signal.
Additionally, a toolbox called Uterine Explorer was developed by 
Batista et al. [33] for processing multichannel EHG signals using WT 
and visualizing the results, achieving the best outcomes with the com-
plex Morlet 15-1 wavelet. In subsequent research, Batista et al. in-
troduced a graphical tool called Contractiongram [15], based on the
Daubechies 10 wavelet, which simplifies the interpretation of EHG data 
recorded throughout pregnancy.
Further advancements in EHG signal processing were made by Ter-
rien et al. [86], who designed a comprehensive interface for processing, 
analyzing, and storing EHG signals. Their system incorporated multiple 
methods, including WT, and supported the processing of EHG signals 
recorded under diverse experimental conditions.
4.3.2. Empirical mode decomposition for signal extraction
Empirical mode decomposition (EMD), like WT, enables the de-
composition of a complex input signal into simpler components called 
intrinsic mode functions (IMFs). These IMFs are typically ordered by 
frequency from highest to lowest. The extracted signal can be ob-
tained by selecting a single IMF or summing multiple suitable IMFs. By 
summing all IMFs, the original input signal can be reconstructed [87].
Li et al. [64] implemented EMD in the wearable fetal monitoring 
system FetalCare for simultaneous fECG and EHG signal recording. The 
EMD algorithm extracted EHG signals by summing the first six IMFs, 
while the fast independent component analysis (FastICA) algorithm 
extracted fECG signals. Testing on three pregnant women during the 
38th–40th week of gestation demonstrated a CCI of 0.8 for uterine 
contraction detection compared to CTG recordings, and an ACC of 
97.97% for fetal QRS (fQRS) complex detection. The system leveraged 
cloud services for data storage and visualization, offering secure and 
low-maintenance functionality.
Taralunga et al. [69] validated EMD for EHG signal extraction 
using synthetic data with varying SNR levels. Synthetic signals were 
generated by combining one record from the TPEHG database [50] 
with interference at five different SNR levels. The average 𝜖 was 0.14. 
The study highlighted that EMD requires no a priori knowledge of the 
signal. Further improvement could be achieved with an automated IMF 
selection algorithm or by combining EMD with other methods.
Hassan et al. [88] combined EMD with canonical correlation anal-
ysis (CCA) to enhance extraction performance. CCA, based on the 
blind source separation principle, separated uterine bursts from noise 
within overlapping frequency bands, while EMD removed residual 
interference. The advantages of this approach included effective in-
terference removal, preservation of the EHG signal’s characteristics, 
computational simplicity, and suitability for preprocessing before EHG 
segmentation.
Chkeir et al. [89] combined EMD with the Savitzky-Golay (SG) filter 
to enhance noise removal. The method was tested on four synthetic and 
one real EHG signal, achieving an SNR of 18.98 dB. The strength of 
EMD lies in its ability to automatically adapt to the characteristics of 
the signal and noise.

4.3.3. Quadratic discriminant analysis for signal extraction
The quadratic discriminant analysis (QDA) classifier is based on the 
supervised learning technique and uses a quadratic decision space. The 
principle of the method is to calculate the distance between the means 
of different classes and then calculate the distance between the mean 
and the sample of each class. Finally, a space with lower dimension is 
created while preserving important information, which maximizes the 
variance between individual classes and minimizes the variance within 
a class [90].
The QDA method was tested by Ye-Lin et al. [52] in classifying 
EHG signal segments into two groups representing artifacted and non-
artifacted segments. In total, eleven spectral, temporal and nonlinear 
signal features were extracted for classification, but the highest accu-
racy (ACC = 92.20%) was achieved when using only seven of them due 
to better generalization ability. Moreover, the QDA method surpassed 
the classification success rate performed by the support vector machine 
(SVM) method with an ACC value of  83.60% and by linear discriminant 
analysis (LDA) with an ACC of  79.40%.
4.4. Uterine contractions detection
After extracting EHG signals, the detection of uterine contractions 
becomes a critical step as they provide valuable information about 
the pregnancy, including the risk of preterm birth, the presence of 
complications, and the progression of labor [61].
In some studies [44,65,80], uterine contractions were detected man-
ually by experts based on significant changes in amplitude and fre-
quency relative to the basal tone. Other studies [47,77,78,81] focused 
on extracting the EHG signal envelope for subsequent analysis. Quan-
titative analysis of EHG parameters has also been used for contraction 
detection, employing metrics such as:
• Amplitude and baseline changes [78].
• Power density spectrum and peak frequency [51].
• Zero-crossing rate and signal power [75].
• Entropy values [91].
• Correlation coefficients [59,79].
Advanced signal processing and ML-based methods have further re-
fined contraction detection, as outlined in this subsection. An overview 
of these methods is provided in Table 6, summarizing the approaches 
and their key outcomes.
4.4.1. Wavelet transform for contractions detection
In addition to its application in EHG signal extraction, the WT 
method, specifically wavelet energy determination, has proven effec-
tive for automatic detection of uterine contractions. Esgalhado et al. [4] 
demonstrated that the Morlet wavelet offers a good compromise be-
tween bandwidth and contraction duration, making it suitable for this 
task.
Moreover, the Morlet wavelet has been effectively used for wavelet 
coherence analysis based on continuous WT. Hassan et al. [56] used 
this approach to detect correlations between bursts of uterine elec-
trical activity recorded from different abdominal locations during the 
same contraction, highlighting the utility of WT in analyzing spatially 
distributed uterine signals.
4.4.2. Multilayer perceptron for contractions detection
Multilayer perceptron (MLP) is a feedforward neural network with 
one or more hidden layers. A characteristic feature of MLP is that each 
neuron in a given layer is fully connected to all neurons in the previous 
layer [92].
In a study by Chendeb et al. [30], MLP was effectively used for 
detecting and classifying EHG signal segments into several categories, 
including contractions, fetal movements, Alvarez waves, LDBF waves, 
and noise. The methodology involved first applying the wavelet packet 
transform (WPT) to decompose the signal into frequency components. 
Subband energies were then calculated and used as features (wavelet 
subband energy detection–WSED) for detecting contractions through 
the dynamic cumulative sum (DCS) method. Finally, MLP was em-
ployed for classification, assigning detected contractions to their respec-
tive categories.
When tested on real data, MLP achieved an ACC of 93.20%, sig-
nificantly outperforming the SVM classifier, which achieved an ACC 
of 79.20%. The study suggested that further improvements could be 
realized by optimizing the selection of wavelet bases.

4.4.3. Convolutional neural network for contractions detection
A convolutional neural network (CNN) is a deep, feedforward, 
multi-layer network with a general structure consisting of an input 
layer, several hidden layers, and an output layer. Hidden layers typ-
ically include convolutional layers, activation layers, pooling layers, 
fully connected layers, and normalization layers. The key advantage 
of CNNs lies in their convolutional layers, which extract important 
features from the input data to create feature maps [95].
In a study by Peng et al. [93], a CNN model with 11 layers 
was developed to detect uterine contractions from EHG signals. Input 
signals were divided into segments of 10 s, 20 s, 30 s, and 60 s. These 
segments were converted into images, normalized, and classified using 
the CNN. The best results (ACC = 88.00%) were achieved using 60 s 
segments when tested on data from the Icelandic database [58].
Hao et al. [94] evaluated a CNN model on both Icelandic database
[58] and 20 real-world recordings. They found the best results with 
45 s segments, achieving an ACC of 93.00% in both cases. One major 
benefit of CNNs highlighted in their study is the elimination of the need 
for separate feature extraction and selection steps, as these processes 
are integrated into the CNN structure. This simplifies signal processing 
compared to conventional ML-based algorithms.
4.4.4. Support vector machine for contractions detection
The primary objective of the support vector machine (SVM) algo-
rithm is to construct a hyperplane that optimally divides the feature 
space. This is achieved by maximizing the minimum distance between 
the hyperplane and the nearest data points on either side, creating the 
widest possible margin around the hyperplane [96].
SVM has been shown to be an effective classifier for detecting EHG 
signal segments that contain or do not contain uterine contractions. In 
a study by Du et al. [46], 112 real recordings from pregnancies at 38 
to 42 weeks of gestation were analyzed. A total of 13 features were 
extracted from the time, frequency, entropy, and nonlinear domains for 
classification. The SVM algorithm with a radial basis function achieved 
an ACC of 85.00%, outperforming other classifiers, including: Decision 
tree (ACC = 79.00%), CNN (ACC = 73.00%), and artificial neural 
network (ANN) with 27 neurons in the hidden layer and sigmoid and 
pureline activation functions (ACC = 75.00%).
4.4.5. Decision trees for contractions detection
The architecture of decision trees consists of nodes and edges, 
where each node represents a signal feature. The root node is the top-
level node with no input edges, marking the beginning of the decision 
process. Internal nodes have one input edge and one or more output 
edges, while leaf nodes have no output edges, representing the end of 
the decision process [97].
Hao et al. [74] tested decision trees for uterine contraction recog-
nition. Fourteen features from the time, frequency, nonlinear, and 
entropy domains were used to classify EHG signal segments as contain-
ing or not containing uterine contractions. The decision tree algorithm 
achieved an ACC of 81.00%. Among the features, power and sample 
entropy were identified as having the greatest influence on accu-
rate contraction detection. Additionally, the placement of the sensing 
electrodes significantly affected detection accuracy, emphasizing the 
importance of proper electrode positioning.
4.4.6. Hierarchical clustering for contractions detection
Hierarchical clustering groups objects based on their similarity, ei-
ther by progressively merging individual objects into larger groups (ag-
glomerative approach) or by dividing larger groups into smaller ones 
(partitioning approach). The result of the analysis is a dendrogram, 
which visually represents the hierarchical structure of the clusters and 
their mutual similarities [98].
Esgalhado et al. [32] applied hierarchical clustering (linkage clus-
tering) for uterine contraction detection. Seven features from the fre-
quency domain were used for clustering, resulting in five distinct 
clusters:
• Cluster 1: Outliers containing a spectral peak at high frequencies.
• Cluster 2: Observations with multiple frequency peaks lacking 
clear physiological interpretation.

4.5. Term/preterm birth prediction
One of the most active areas of research in the field of EHG is 
the classification of term and preterm recordings. Earlier studies of 
EHG signals [18] revealed significant electrophysiological changes in 
the uterus preceding spontaneous preterm labor. These changes are 
characterized by more intense contractions, a greater prevalence of 
higher frequencies, faster and more coordinated activity propagation, 
and stronger interconnections between different uterine regions. Tem-
poral, spectral, nonlinear, and bivariate analyses of EHG signals thus 
provide valuable information for predicting preterm labor.
For the classification of term and preterm recordings, some re-
searchers [48,65,82,99] employed statistical methods that compared 
features extracted from the frequency, time, entropy, nonlinear, or 
information-theoretic domains.
A growing number of studies have focused on implementing AI and 
ML-based algorithms, as summarized in Table 7. These approaches offer 
the potential for faster and more accurate preterm labor predictions, 
which can be critical in emergency situations where timely medical 
intervention is essential [100].
4.5.1. Artificial neural network for preterm birth prediction
Artificial neural networks (ANNs) are computational models in-
spired by the way the human brain processes information. ANNs consist 
of artificial neurons connected in a structured network, where each 
neuron receives input signals, modifies them using weight coefficients 
and a transfer function, and passes the processed signals onward. 
Through learning, the network adjusts these weights to effectively solve 
specific tasks based on prior experience [101].
Diab et al. [41] employed a simple perceptron for the binary classi-
fication of term and preterm EHG recordings. The optimal input to the 
ANN consisted of 10 features derived from the frequency domain. Using 
fewer features resulted in a high error rate. The method was tested 
on 20 real recordings and 30 simulated recordings, achieving an ACC 
of 96.70%. The authors suggested that using a multilayer perceptron 
(MLP) could further improve classification accuracy.
Naeem et al. [102] tested a traditional feedforward backpropaga-
tion ANN for term/preterm classification. They evaluated 14 features 
from the time, frequency, entropy, and nonlinear domains, identify-
ing 8 entropy- and nonlinear-based features as the most effective. 
The traditional ANN achieved an ACC of 92.30%, outperforming both 
the cascade-forward backpropagation ANN (ACC = 87.70%) and the 
Kohonen self-organizing ANN (ACC = 68.50%).
4.5.2. Multilayer perceptron for preterm birth prediction
Beyond its application in detecting uterine contractions, the MLP 
has also proven effective for classifying term/preterm EHG recordings. 
In a study by Mas-Cabo et al. [103], an MLP model with a hyperbolic 
tangent activation function and a backpropagation training algorithm 
was employed. In order to reduce feature dimensionality and pre-
vent overfitting, principal component analysis (PCA) was used. The 
study used unbalanced data from the TPEHG database [50], which 
was balanced using the synthetic minority over-sampling technique 
(SMOTE) [104].
A total of 72 features were extracted, encompassing time, frequency, 
and nonlinear domains of the EHG signal, as well as 5 clinical features 
(e.g., maternal age, parity, previous abortions). Among 10 proposed 
models, the best-performing variant used a multi-channel approach 
incorporating 16 signal features and 5 clinical features, achieving an 
ACC of 87.90%. The inclusion of clinical data significantly improved 
classification accuracy. For this reasons, the authors recommended 
incorporating additional obstetrical markers, such as cervical length, 
fetal fibronectin, or interleukin 6, in future research to further enhance 
classification performance.
4.5.3. Spiking neural network for preterm birth prediction
Spiking neural networks (SNNs) are a type of network that does not 
process data using continuous values, but works with discrete impulses 
or spikes. Neurons only emit spikes when a certain threshold potential 
is reached, which saves energy and allows for more efficient real-time 
information processing [105].
Veena et al. [83] implemented SNNs within an automated preterm 
prediction system. The metaheuristic seagull optimization algorithm 
(SOA) was used to optimize the SNN parameters. Using four features 
from the time, frequency, and entropy domains, ACC = 98.35% was 
achieved, which surpassed the performance of decision tree:
ACC = 90.53%, k-nearest neighbors (k-NN): ACC = 93.21%, and SVM: 
ACC = 95.41%.
4.5.4. Convolution neural network for preterm birth prediction
Convolution neural network has proven to be a universal approach 
suitable for both uterine contraction detection and term/preterm EHG 
record classification, as shown in the study by Deng et al. [106]. It 
was a very deep CNN with VGG-Net architecture requiring a large 
amount of data for effective operation. Therefore, it was combined 
with transfer learning (fine tune technique) pre-trained on large sample 
data. Recurrence plot analysis was also implemented to obtain the 
nonlinear structural information characteristics in high-dimensional 
space. The approach was tested on the TPEHG database [50], which 
achieved ACC = 97.00% and F1 = 96.97% for the validation data set. 
Data imbalance was solved through a specific approach that involved 
selecting equal numbers of term and preterm EHG data for training 
and testing. The advantage of CNN was the simplification of feature 
extraction and selection, when these steps are part of the CNN structure.
4.5.5. Temporal convolution network for preterm birth prediction
A temporal convolution network (TCN) is a specialized type of 
CNN designed for processing temporal sequences. Unlike recurrent 
methods, TCNs utilize convolutional layers to process all time steps 
simultaneously, making them more efficient at capturing long-term 
dependencies [107].
Fischer et al. [108] employed a TCN to predict preterm birth using 
an end-to-end learning approach with interpretability. The model was 
tested on unbalanced data from the TPEHG database [50]. Instead 
of oversampling to balance the data, the authors implemented cost-
sensitive learning by adjusting class weights in the loss function. The 
best performance was achieved by the TCN model combined with 7 
clinical features and sample entropy, yielding an AUC of 0.578. This 
significantly outperformed the long short-term memory (LSTM) model, 
which achieved an AUC of 0.423 with the same feature set.
4.5.6. Radial basis function network for preterm birth prediction
Radial basis function network (RBFN) is a special type of feedfor-
ward network containing only three layers (input, hidden, and output) 
and a radial basis as an activation function. The advantage of the 
network is universal approximation and higher learning rate [126].
RBFN was shown to be the most suitable solution among six other 
ANNs for detecting preterm births in a study by Fergus et al. [109].

4.5.7. Recurrent neural network for preterm birth prediction
Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) are fully connected networks 
that utilize cyclic connections, enabling neurons to retain information 
about previous inputs. This architecture allows for bidirectional infor-
mation flow, where signals propagate both forward and backward—
from higher layers to lower layers [127].
Goldsztejn et al. [110] proposed a deep learning approach for pre-
dicting preterm births, combining RNN with bidirectional long short-
term memory (BILSTM). Short-time Fourier transform (STFT) was ap-
plied to the EHG signals, transforming them into a time–frequency 
representation that was used as input to the network. A key advantage 
of this architecture was its ability to automatically learn relevant 
frequency-domain features directly from the data, eliminating the need 
for handcrafted feature extraction.
The model was tested on the TPEHG [50] and TPEHGT [66] 
datasets, using a weighted cross-entropy loss function (WCELF) to ad-
dress data imbalance. Incorporating clinical data into the classification 
model improved the area under the curve (AUC) value from 0.740 to 
0.780. Additionally, spectral features were found to be more predictive 
than temporal features. To further enhance prediction accuracy, the 
inclusion of additional clinical features, such as cervical length or 
fibronectin alpha, was recommended.

and TPEHGT [66], where in the former the data was balanced by select-
ing an equal number of term and preterm records. A total of 33 features 
were extracted from the time, frequency, time–frequency and entropy 
domains, and 7–13 suitable features were selected. It was shown that 
especially WT and entropy-based features can be suitable descriptors 
for the analysis of EHG signals. The best results (ACC = 88.52%) were 
achieved by the quadratic SVM variant. On the contrary, in the study by

A similar comparison of three different kernel functions of SVM

The SVM algorithm is often combined with other algorithms to 
improve performance, especially in term/preterm EHG recording clas-
sification. This is primarily because SVM, on its own, lacks mechanisms 
to identify the most relevant features for classification. By combining it 
with other algorithms, it becomes possible to select an optimal subset 
of features, thereby reducing redundancy and improving accuracy.
• Particle swarm optimization (PSO) — Acharya et al. [100] com-
bined SVM with a radial basis function kernel and the PSO 
algorithm to select eight significant features, evaluated using the 
Bhattacharyya technique. This approach achieved high classifica-
tion accuracy (ACC = 96.25%) and was noted for its simplicity 
and cost-effectiveness.
• Sequential forward selection (SFS) – Cheng et al. [61] combined 
SVM with SFS to identify the optimal feature set, achieving an 
ACC of 90.50%. The study also revealed that using the entire EHG 
signal outperformed analyzing individual contraction segments.
• Signal decomposition methods — Far et al. [112] used EMD to 
decompose input signals and extracted three features from the 
first two IMFs for SVM classification. This method achieved an 
impressive ACC of 99.70%, outperforming EMD-decision tree 
(ACC = 98.70%) and EMD-k-NN (ACC = 96.60%). Moreover, 
Janjarasjitt et al. [111] applied EMD and WT for signal decompo-
sition and time-domain feature extraction, enhancing prediction 
accuracy to ACC = 96.19% (EMD-SFS-SVM) and ACC = 97.76% 
(WT-SFS-SVM). Finally, Sadi et al. [116] applied HHT for signal 
decomposition and used linear SVM with two IMFs, achiev-
ing ACC = 95.70%. This low-complexity approach was deemed 
suitable for outpatient and telemedicine applications.
• Recurrence quantification analysis (RQA) — Borowska et al. [49] 
combined SVM with RQA for feature extraction but achieved 
lower accuracy (ACC = 79.27%). Adding PCA algorithm improved 
results slightly to ACC = 83.32%.
• Time–frequency domain and horizontal visibility graph (HVG) – 
Xu et al. [113] used HVG to convert EHG signals into graph 
representations, extracting 152 features. The best results (ACC =
91.00%) were obtained using SVM with a radial basis function 
and optimized parameters.

4.5.9. Adaptive boosting for preterm birth prediction
Adaptive boosting (AdaBoost) is a boosting method that creates a 
strong classifier by combining multiple weaker models. It iteratively 
focuses on difficult-to-classify cases by adjusting the weights of training 
examples, and the final prediction is determined through a weighted 
vote [128].
Ren et al. [117] applied AdaBoost for term/preterm classification, 
using entropy-based features from 10 IMFs obtained via EMD. The 
study, which used SMOTE [104] for data balancing, achieved the high-
est AUC (0.986) compared to other tested classifiers, such as random 
forest (AUC = 0.957), Bayesian network (AUC = 0.912), simple logistic 
regression (SLR) (AUC = 0.874), and SVM (AUC = 0.847).
Far et al. [118] used AdaBoost on data from the TPEHG database, 
dividing records into two groups based on gestational age (before and 
after 26 weeks). The classifier achieved high accuracy for both groups: 
ACC = 95.00% (before 26 weeks) and ACC = 93.00% (after 26 weeks). 
The self-adaptive synthetic over-sampling method (SASYNO) [129] was 
employed to balance the dataset, and features were extracted from 
time, frequency, entropy, and nonlinear domains.
4.5.10. Random forest for preterm birth prediction
Random forest is an ensemble method that combines multiple de-
cision trees, each trained on a different random sample of data and a 
randomly selected subset of variables. For classification tasks, predic-
tions are made through majority voting, which enhances accuracy and 
stability while reducing the risk of overfitting [130].
Chowdhury et al. [119] applied random forest for term/preterm 
EHG signal classification, testing on the TPEHG [50] and TPEHGT [66] 
databases. Using the SMOTE technique [104] for balancing and six 
features from the time, entropy, and clinical domains, the model 
achieved an ACC of 89.00%. The authors recommended expanding 
future datasets to include other delivery types, such as induced and 
cesarean deliveries, to broaden the model’s applicability.
In a study by You et al. [120], random forest was combined with 
multivariate empirical mode decomposition (MEMD). In this combina-
tion, random forest achieved an ACC of 93.18%, though it was slightly 
outperformed by the random subspace (RandSub) algorithm, which 
reached an ACC of 94.66%.
4.5.11. Linear discriminant analysis for preterm birth prediction
The objective of the linear discriminant analysis (LDA) classifier 
is to find a linear combination of features that effectively separates 
individual classes. This is achieved by transforming the data to max-
imize variability between classes while minimizing variability within 
classes [90].
In a study by Nieto-del-Amor et al. [121], LDA was combined with 
a genetic algorithm (GA) for feature selection, optimizing classification 
performance by reducing redundancy and maximizing the information 
provided by selected features. Testing was conducted on data from the
TPEHG [50] and TPEHGT [66] databases, balanced using the SMOTE 
technique [104]. Out of 46 extracted features, GA identified 12 optimal 
features from the frequency, entropy, nonlinear, and clinical domains. 
The study highlighted the significance of entropy measures, particu-
larly bubble and dispersion entropy, which outperformed conventional 
linear features.
In a comparative study [122], GA-LDA achieved an ACC of 88.77%, 
outperforming GA combined with logistic regression (LR) (ACC =
87.42%) and GA-k-NN (ACC = 82.92%). However, in their follow-up 
study [123], the authors noted that SMOTE [104] might overestimate 
the generalization ability of models. They tested GA-LDA with various 
resampling techniques, including undersampling, oversampling, and 
hybrid methods. The combination of GA-LDA with undersampling 
was found to be the most effective, providing the most accurate 
identification of preterm births.
4.5.12. Quadratic discriminant analysis for preterm birth prediction
QDA method has demonstrated effectiveness in classifying term 
versus preterm births, as highlighted by Saleem et al. [124]. This 
study quantified the coupling between electrical (EHG) and mechanical 
(TOCO) uterine activity by analyzing signals during both contracting 
and non-contracting (dummy) intervals. Six time-domain features, in-
cluding Granger causality and directionality indices, were used for 
classification. The results showed higher accuracy when features from 
both EHG and TOCO signals were combined (ACC = 91.00%) compared 
to using only EHG features (ACC = 84.00%). The authors recommended 
exploring shorter recording segments to enhance clinical applicability.
Jager et al. [66] further investigated QDA in combination with 
SFS for the selection of relevant features, and found that analyzing 
dummy intervals yielded comparable or slightly better classification 
performance than contracting intervals, suggesting the utility of these 
intervals for predictive modeling.
4.5.13. Gaussian mixture model for preterm birth prediction
The Gaussian mixture model (GMM) is a probabilistic model that 
assumes data samples originate from a mixture of finite Gaussian 
distributions. It assigns samples to classes based on their probability 
of belonging to individual Gaussian components [131].
Shahrdad et al. [125] applied GMM to data from the TPEHG
database [50], using the boosting by random undersampling (RUS-
Boost) method to address dataset imbalance. Linear predictive coding 
(LPC) was used for feature extraction, yielding eight features from 
the frequency domain (8th-order LPC coefficients). The classification 
results were influenced by incomplete clinical data, which was missing 
for some records. Separate analyses were conducted based on the 
availability of clinical information, with higher accuracy achieved for 
data with clinical information (ACC = 89.00%) compared to data 
without clinical information (ACC = 74.30%).
4.6. Labor/non-labor records classification
Analyzing the characteristics of uterine contractions can provide 
valuable insights into distinguishing labor from non-labor states. Simi-
lar to term/preterm classification, this task has been approached using 
statistical methods that compare features from the time and entropy 
domains [65,76], as well as nonlinear correlation analysis [132]. This 
subsection highlights studies where AI and ML-based algorithms were 
applied, as summarized in Table 8.
4.6.1. Extreme learning machine for labor classification
Extreme learning machine (ELM) is a feedforward network with a 
single hidden layer. Unlike traditional ANNs, which iteratively optimize 
all parameters, ELM randomly sets hidden neuron weights and opti-
mizes only the output layer. This enables high learning speed while 
maintaining generalization ability [133].
Chen et al. [134] applied ELM for pregnancy/labor EHG classifi-
cation, combining it with EMD and HHT methods. EMD decomposed 
EHG signals into three IMFs, and HHT was applied to IMF1 to derive 
an analytical function and its amplitude. This single feature was used 
for classification with an ELM containing 20 neurons and a sigmoidal 
transfer function. The approach achieved an ACC of 88.00%, excelling 
in speed and generalization compared to traditional algorithms.
4.6.2. Support vector machine for labor classification
SVM with a radial basis function kernel was effective for labor 
prediction in a study by Cheng et al. [61], achieving an ACC of 
96.40%. SFS algorithm identified the optimal feature set, narrowing 
131 extracted features to 5–11 time-, frequency-, and entropy-based 
features. Data balancing was achieved through undersampling, and 
novel cross-entropy and multichannel entropy features significantly 
improved classification accuracy for labor/non-labor records.

4.6.3. Random forest for labor classification
Altini et al. [60] demonstrated the suitability of random forest 
for labor/non-labor classification using a wearable sensor. The study 
evaluated various feature combinations:
• Gestational age only: ACC = 68.00%.
• EHG features only: ACC = 71.00%.
• mHR features only: ACC = 71.00%.
• EHG + mHR features: ACC = 82.00%.
• All three (gestational age, EHG, and mHR): ACC = 87.00%.
Longer signal segments (20 min) enabled effective pattern detection 
of uterine contractions without manual segmentation.
4.6.4. Quadratic discriminant analysis for labor classification
QDA was identified as the best-performing algorithm for labor/preg-
nancy contraction classification in a comparative study by Alame-
dine et al. [57]. Using 10 features selected via binary PSO (BPSO), 
QDA achieved an ACC of 88.72%, outperforming: (1) BPSO with k-NN: 
ACC = 84.96% and (2) BPSO with LDA: ACC = 83.71%. Key features 
included variance entropy and the eighth decile of power spectral 
density. In a follow-up study [135], the most discriminating features 
identified were the nonlinear correlation coefficient, sample entropy, 
variance entropy, and the first decile of power spectral density.
4.6.5. Gaussian mixture model for labor classification
Tylcz et al. [59] applied GMM to classify EHG signal segments 
as imminent labor or pregnancy. Model parameters were optimized 
using the Bayesian information criterion. The dataset, consisting of 
68 real records, was balanced using SMOTE [104] to address the 
overrepresentation of pregnancy samples. Five features from the time, 
frequency, entropy, and nonlinear domains were used, achieving an 
ACC of 76.20%. However, false detections caused by artifacts signifi-
cantly impacted overall accuracy, highlighting the need for improved 
artifact removal in future studies.
5. Summary of evidence
This section summarizes the principal findings in the field of EHG 
signal processing and analysis, with particular emphasis on the re-
ported performance of classification algorithms and the signal features 
most frequently used in the literature. In addition, a comparison of 
the performance across different feature domains is provided, along 
with a discussion of how selected features may reflect underlying 
physiological or pathological phenomena. 
5.1. Algorithms’ performance
A comparative evaluation of the most effective computational meth-
ods in EHG research is presented in Table 9. This table summarizes the 
top-performing algorithms across three primary application domains: 
uterine contraction detection, term versus preterm birth prediction, and 
classification of labor versus non-labor records. The included methods 
were evaluated using publicly available datasets, with ACC metrics 
reported as presented in the original studies.
In the domain of uterine contraction detection, both the WT-based 
approach [4] and the CNN-based method [93] demonstrated strong 
performance, achieving accuracy rates of 92.28% and 88.00%, re-
spectively, on the Icelandic dataset. These results indicate that both 
traditional signal processing techniques and deep learning models can 
be highly effective for this task. The WT method benefits from its ca-
pacity to extract time–frequency characteristics that are well-suited for 
identifying the non-stationary patterns of uterine contractions. Mean-
while, the CNN-based model leverages its ability to automatically learn 
discriminative features directly from the data, making it a competitive 
alternative.
In the context of preterm birth prediction, a number of computa-
tional methods have demonstrated high performance across different 
EHG datasets. The results can be meaningfully grouped according to the 
dataset used: TPEHG, TPEHGT, and a combined dataset (TPEHG + TPE-
HGT). The TPEHG dataset has yielded the highest reported accuracies. 
The standard SVM-based classifier presented in [115] achieved 99.74% 
accuracy, making it the top-performing method overall. Similarly, the 
BPF-EMD-SVM hybrid model in [112] reached an accuracy of 99.70%. 
Additionally, the BPF, EMD, and decision tree-based approach [112] 
also demonstrated strong results, with an accuracy of 98.70%. Us-
ing the extended TPEHGT dataset, the hybrid Wiener filter-SOA-SNN 
developed in [83] achieved an accuracy of 98.35%, followed by a 
standard SVM classifier combined with a Wiener filter from the same 
study, which reached 95.41%. When both datasets were combined, 
the performance slightly decreased. The BPF-GA-LDA method [121] 
achieved 89.20%, and the BPF-random forest classifier [119] reached 
89.00%. 
In the area of labor versus non-labor classification, two computa-
tional methods have demonstrated comparably strong performance on 
the Icelandic dataset. The BPF-BPSO-QDA approach [57] achieved the 
highest accuracy of 88.72%, closely followed by the BPF-HHT-ELM 
method [134] with an accuracy of 88.00%.
Overall, the reviewed methods demonstrated that both classical ML 
models and advanced hybrid or deep learning approaches can achieve 
high accuracy across different EHG classification tasks. These find-
ings emphasize the need for continued benchmarking on standardized

5.2. Significant EHG features
Based on the reviewed literature, several categories of features have 
proven to be significant for achieving high classification accuracy in 
EHG-based algorithms. These features were derived from multiple do-
mains, including time, frequency, time–frequency, entropy, nonlinear, 
and clinical. A summary of these significant features is presented in 
Table 10. This table provides an overview of the research areas where 
these features have been used, listing the specific studies that presented 
their use.
The time domain features focused on capturing the amplitude, vari-
ability, and temporal trends of the EHG signal. Metrics such as root 
mean square, log detector, mean absolute value, difference absolute 
standard deviation, and average amplitude change were fundamental 
in classification tasks, offering essential insights into uterine activity. 
Among all feature categories, time-domain features were the most 
consistently used across high-performing classification models and con-
tributed significantly to prediction accuracy. These features effectively 
reflected the strength and duration of uterine contractions, typically 
increasing as labor approached.
In the frequency domain, key features such as median, mean, and 
peak frequency were among the most influential. Frequency-domain 
features were commonly integrated alongside time-domain metrics, 
providing complementary information. They were associated with phys-
iological phenomena such as the firing rate of myometrial cells and the 
propagation speed of action potentials. Higher frequency components 
were generally linked to term labor and effective uterine contractions, 
whereas lower frequency components were more indicative of preterm 
or disorganized uterine activity.
The time–frequency domain leveraged methods such as the HHT, 
STFT, and WT to capture both temporal and spectral characteristics 
of the EHG signal. Although less frequently employed, these features 
enabled detailed analyses of the temporal evolution of signal energy 
across different frequency bands. This facilitated the detection of tran-
sient bursts and nonstationary changes in uterine activity, offering a 
more comprehensive representation of contraction dynamics.
In the nonlinear domain, features were designed to capture the 
complex, chaotic, or fractal nature of uterine electrical activity, which 
linear methods often fail to address. Metrics such as time reversibility, 
the Lyapunov exponent, and Katz’s fractal dimension provided valuable 
insights, particularly for identifying irregular or unpredictable con-
traction patterns. These features described the intrinsic dynamics of 
uterine signals and demonstrated strong predictive power, especially 
in detecting abnormal behaviors associated with preterm labor.
Entropy-based features quantified the complexity and irregularity 
of the EHG signal. Measures such as sample entropy and fuzzy en-
tropy were particularly effective in characterizing the non-stationary 
nature of uterine contractions. While entropy-based features rarely 
outperformed other domains individually, they contributed meaningful 
complementary information when combined with other feature types. 
Higher entropy values were typically observed in early pregnancy, re-
flecting disorganized and asynchronous uterine activity, whereas lower 
values near term indicated more synchronized and regular contractions.
Lastly, clinical features provided important contextual information 
that enhanced the performance of classification models. Variables such 
as gestational age, maternal age, parity, and history of abortions were 
commonly included. Although used in a smaller number of studies, 
their integration significantly improved predictive accuracy by linking 
EHG-derived signals to specific physiological or pathological states, 
such as preterm labor. Table 11 provides an overview of the main 
feature domains analyzed and their physiological relevance.
In conclusion, models that integrated features from multiple
domains—particularly time, frequency, and nonlinear—achieved the 
highest predictive performance. This underscores the importance of 
multimodal feature fusion in the classification of labor and the pre-
diction of preterm birth.
6. Discussion
Although many recent studies have demonstrated encouraging re-
sults in signal extraction, uterine contractions detection, preterm birth 
prediction, and labor classification using EHG signals, these outcomes 
do not yet guarantee clinical readiness. Both methodological limitations 
and key decisions regarding analysis strategies need to be addressed 
prior to clinical or wearable deployment.

• Limited and Unbalanced Data Availability — Another critical limi-
tation of current ML models is their frequent application to small 
and imbalanced datasets. Publicly available EHG datasets typi-
cally comprise a limited number of samples, with a marked un-
derrepresentation of pathological cases—such as preterm births—
relative to normal term pregnancies. This class imbalance can lead 
to biased models that disproportionately favor the majority class, 
thereby distorting the estimation of actual clinical risk. Several 
studies have attempted to address this issue using various data 
balancing strategies, including oversampling techniques such as 
SMOTE [59,103,109,117,119–122] and ADASYN [66,100,115], 
as well as undersampling approaches [61,116,123] to equalize 
class distributions. However, the impact of these techniques on 
model generalization is seldom evaluated in a standardized or 
reproducible manner. Moreover, the risk of overfitting remains 
high, particularly when models are trained and validated on small 
or non-independent subsets of data, often relying solely on k-fold 
cross-validation instead of evaluation on an independent hold-out 
test set. Crucially, external validation—i.e., testing on completely 
unseen data from a different population or institution—is rarely 
performed, which significantly limits the generalizability and 
translational potential of the proposed models.
• Models’ Interpretability – Despite its recognized importance for 
building clinical trust in AI systems, interpretability remains con-
spicuously absent from current EHG-based studies. While most 
current studies focus on maximizing classification performance, 
only a limited number have integrated explainable artificial intel-
ligence (XAI) techniques to elucidate decision-making processes. 
For instance, Kuppala et al. [136] applied Shapley additive ex-
planations (SHAP) to a GraphSAGE-based classifier for preterm 
birth prediction. Similarly, Kang et al. [137] explored phase 
synchronization between EHG and TOCO signals for preterm birth 
prediction, using SHAP to interpret the contribution of individual 
mean phase coherence features to the ML model’s predictions. 
These initial efforts underscore the growing recognition of XAI 
as a critical component in advancing both the transparency and 
clinical applicability of EHG-based predictive models.
• Challenges Hindering Clinical Translation – Despite significant
progress in EHG signal acquisition, signal processing, and ML-
based classification, the clinical translation of EHG into rou-
tine obstetric care remains limited. Multiple interrelated barri-
ers contribute to this translational gap. One of the major lim-
itations is the absence of standardized and clinically endorsed 
protocols for electrode placement, signal acquisition, prepro-
cessing, and interpretation. This lack of harmonized guidelines 
results in substantial variability across studies and hinders the 
development of unified clinical recommendations. Consequently, 
obstetricians often lack clear decision-making frameworks for 
incorporating EHG data into clinical practice, leading to hes-
itancy in adopting the technology due to concerns about di-
agnostic responsibility and potential medicolegal consequences 
in the absence of validated guidelines and formal training. In 
addition, current EHG-based systems are rarely integrated with 
existing obstetric monitoring infrastructure, making their deploy-
ment challenging—particularly in low-resource settings, where 
ease of use and compatibility are critical. Most algorithms are 
also not implemented in real-time processing hardware, which 
further limits their clinical applicability. Finally, the majority 
of current studies rely on small, often imbalanced or synthet-
ically augmented datasets that fail to reflect the full spectrum 
of physiological and pathological uterine activity across diverse 
populations and gestational stages. This severely limits both 
model generalizability and clinical credibility. Moreover, the 
interpretability of ML models remains inadequate for clinical 
settings, where transparent and explainable decision-making is 
essential to gain physician acceptance and trust.
• Technical Challenges in Practical Deployment – Beyond these clin-
ical and methodological barriers, further technical constraints 
arise from computational and hardware limitations. Many current 
models, especially deep neural networks, exceed the process-
ing power, memory capacity, and energy budgets of embed-
ded and portable platforms. Real-time operation under such con-
straints demands hardware-aware optimization—such as pruning, 
quantization, and streamlined inference—to reduce latency and 
power consumption without degrading accuracy [138]. Algorithm 
choice must match device capabilities: lightweight ML models are 
viable for low-power devices, whereas deep architectures require 
high-performance edge or cloud infrastructure. Moreover, current 
EHG datasets do not yet meet the technical data requirements 
for large-scale deployment—they remain small, heterogeneous, 
and often lack standardized recording formats or consistent label-
ing, which complicates model generalization, signal fusion, and 
benchmarking across devices [139].
7. Future directions
Although recent studies have shown encouraging progress in EHG 
signal acquisition, processing, and classification, several overarching 
research gaps still limit the generalizability and clinical applicability 
of current systems:
1. Data and methodological standardization: Publicly available
datasets remain limited in population diversity, clinical settings, 
and obstetric risk profiles. In addition, variability in contraction 
annotation, artifact labeling, electrode placement, and acquisi-
tion protocols hinders reproducibility and direct comparison of 
results.
2. Model transparency and validation: Many high-performing mod-
els, particularly deep learning architectures, lack interpretabil-
ity, which limits clinician trust. External validation across in-
dependent, diverse cohorts is also rare, reducing confidence in 
model generalizability.
3. Clinical and technical translation: Current systems are seldom 
integrated into obstetric workflows or compatible with existing 
monitoring infrastructure. Real-time feasibility and optimiza-
tion for wearable or embedded platforms remain underexplored, 
hindering routine clinical deployment.
Addressing these gaps will require coordinated multicenter efforts to 
establish standardized acquisition and validation frameworks, develop 
interpretable and trustworthy models, and ensure practical integration 
into clinical workflows. These priorities form the foundation for the 
research directions outlined below.
• Ensure Standardization – The current methodology for EHG lacks 
uniform guidelines, particularly regarding the number and place-
ment of sensing electrodes. Additionally, inconsistent use of test 
records and evaluation approaches for algorithm performance 
complicates comparisons across studies and limits clinical appli-
cability. Standardized protocols for measurement and analysis 
are essential to ensure consistency, facilitate clinical translation, 
and advance EHG’s adoption into routine practice. Importantly, 
such standardization could serve as the foundation for develop-
ing clinically validated guidelines and formal recommendations,

which are currently lacking. Their absence contributes to hes-
itancy among healthcare providers to adopt EHG, due to con-
cerns about diagnostic responsibility and potential medicolegal 
consequences.
• Extend Data Collection – Despite the availability of four pub-
licly accessible datasets, the field suffers from a lack of large-
scale, high-quality, and well-annotated data encompassing a wide 
range of physiological and pathological conditions across differ-
ent stages of pregnancy and labor. Addressing these limitations 
is crucial for robust algorithm validation. Moreover, the reliance 
on synthetic data generated via oversampling techniques like 
SMOTE [104] introduces potential biases and overestimation of 
classifier performance [19,112]. Future efforts should prioritize 
collecting diverse, balanced datasets to improve reliability. In this 
context, multicenter prospective studies involving geographically 
and demographically diverse populations are essential to ensure 
generalizability and clinical relevance of developed algorithms. 
Furthermore, federated learning frameworks [140] offer a promis-
ing approach to enable collaborative model training across insti-
tutions without the need for direct data sharing, thus addressing 
both data privacy concerns and the challenge of data heterogene-
ity. Integrating these strategies may significantly accelerate the 
clinical translation of EHG-based diagnostic systems.
• Deploy Multimodal Integration and Signal Fusion – Integration of 
EHG with complementary physiological signals offers significant 
potential for improving predictive accuracy and enabling context-
aware pregnancy monitoring [141]. Among the most common 
combinations are electrical signals – NI-fECG and mECG, which 
are readily accessible from abdominal recordings along with EHG 
component and provide concurrent information on fHR, mHR, 
and uterine activity without requiring additional sensors [12,13] 
Similarly, maternal respiration can be estimated from the same 
abdominal recordings by isolating low-frequency baseline modu-
lations (typically 0.1–1 Hz) using envelope extraction or filtering 
techniques. Beyond cardiac and respiratory signals, additional 
wearable modalities such as maternal oxygen saturation (SpO2), 
body temperature, and mobility/activity monitoring can provide 
valuable context for interpreting uterine electrical activity. For 
example, changes in SpO2 or temperature can reflect maternal or 
fetal stress, while accelerometry-based mobility monitoring can 
help identify motion artifacts [142]. Optical techniques, including 
near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) [143] and transabdominal fetal 
pulse oximetry (TfPO) [144,145], can monitor placental and fetal 
oxygenation, while acoustic modalities such as fPCG [146,147] 
provide non-invasive access to fHR and breathing patterns. Com-
bining these modalities with EHG requires effective signal fusion 
strategies. Feature-level fusion combines features from each modal-
ity prior to classification, capturing complementary physiological 
information, whereas decision-level fusion aggregates independent 
outputs from separate models to improve robustness against noise 
or missing channels. However, several technical challenges must 
be addressed: (i) synchronization of signals with different sam-
pling rates, (ii) mitigation of cross-interference between overlap-
ping maternal, fetal, and uterine signals, (iii) standardization of 
heterogeneous clinical data, and (iv) computational and power 
constraints for real-time fusion in wearable devices.
• Include Additional Clinical Features – Feature selection plays a 
critical role in the development of accurate classification models. 
In the context of EHG signal analysis, features from the time, 
frequency, entropy, and nonlinear domains have been commonly 
employed. Moreover, the integration of clinical features has sig-
nificantly improved classification accuracy in various studies [60,
103,108,109,121–123]. However, these features have been un-
derutilized. Incorporating additional obstetrical markers, such as 
cervical length, fetal fibronectin, interleukin-6, and fibronectin 
alpha, could further enhance predictive models [103,110]. Future 
datasets should prioritize including these markers to improve clas-
sification precision. Additionally, exploring other yet unexplored 
feature domains could further advance the field and enable more 
comprehensive predictive systems.
• Expand Clinical Applications – As part of the efforts to expand 
clinical applications, a particularly promising direction for future 
research is the use of EHG in evaluating and predicting outcomes 
related to labor induction. Several recent studies [148–150] have 
explored EHG features as biomarkers of uterine responsiveness to 
pharmacological or mechanical induction methods. For instance, 
Benalcazar et al. [148] analyzed EHG signals to assess the uterine 
response to Misoprostol and Dinoprostone using temporal, spec-
tral, and complexity parameters. They identified signal markers 
that were predictive of successful labor induction, particularly 
in cases involving Misoprostol. In a subsequent study [149], 
the same group developed ANN-based prediction systems that 
combined EHG and obstetric features to predict induction success 
across three clinical scenarios. Their results demonstrated that 
EHG features selected via PSO outperformed traditional obstet-
ric parameters and enabled early prediction of successful in-
duction. In another study [150], a SVM-based approach with 
majority voting aggregation was developed to predict whether 
term pregnancies would result in spontaneous labor or require 
late-term induction. The inclusion of obstetrical context alongside 
EHG features significantly improved prediction accuracy. Another 
promising direction involves intrapartum applications of EHG. 
Beyond predicting the outcome of labor induction, EHG may also 
be used to monitor the electrophysiological response of the uterus 
during labor itself. This can help identify abnormal progression 
patterns, such as labor arrest, and may enable earlier clinical 
interventions aimed at improving maternal and fetal outcomes. 
Taken together, these findings point to the untapped potential of 
EHG in guiding individualized labor management.
• Design Hardware-Aware Algorithms – For real-time clinical de-
ployment, it is essential not only to develop computationally 
efficient algorithms, but also to consider their compatibility with 
target hardware platforms from the early stages of development. 
While many high-performing models have been evaluated in of-
fline settings using standard PCs or cloud infrastructures, their 
translation to embedded or wearable systems imposes constraints 
such as limited memory, reduced processing power, and en-
ergy consumption requirements. Future research should therefore 
focus on hardware-aware optimization strategies, including prun-
ing, quantization, or low-power event-driven processing, which 
can significantly reduce computational demands and enable real-
time execution on constrained platforms. Additionally, the de-
velopment of standardized real-time processing pipelines and 
benchmarking tools would facilitate objective comparisons across 
studies and platforms, thereby accelerating the clinical translation 
of EHG-based monitoring systems.
• Develop Automatic EHG Segmentation – Another promising area of 
exploration is the application of deep learning for the automatic 
segmentation and interpretation of EHG signals. Traditional anal-
ysis methods often rely on manual or semi-automated processing, 
which can be time-consuming, subjective, and difficult to scale 
in clinical settings. In contrast, recent developments [151] in 
semantic segmentation techniques enable the identification of 
physiologically relevant EHG components—such as bursts, base-
lines, or noise segments—directly from raw data. These models 
can improve signal interpretability, enhance robustness against 
artifacts, and reduce the burden on clinical staff. Ultimately, such 
tools open the way for real-time, automated EHG monitoring 
systems that could support clinical decision-making and remote 
care applications.

• Enhancing Trust and Interpretability – To facilitate widespread 
clinical adoption of AI-driven systems in obstetrics, particularly 
those based on EHG, models must be not only accurate but also 
interpretable and trustworthy. Simple systems based on familiar 
guidelines are easier for clinicians to understand, while more 
complex deep learning-based systems often lack transparency. 
This ‘‘black box’’ nature poses significant challenges for health-
care applications, where decisions must be explainable and defen-
sible. To overcome this limitation, future research should focus on 
integrating XAI techniques that provide human-understandable 
justifications for model outputs. Moreover, large language models 
(LLMs) should be incorporated into the clinical decision-making 
workflow as interpretable interfaces between clinicians and com-
plex EHG classifiers. These models should be used to generate 
natural-language explanations of AI outputs, contextualize pre-
dictions with clinical narratives, and assist with summarizing 
signal patterns and risk profiles. Such approaches should enhance 
clinical trust, transparency, and overall clinical usability.
8. Conclusion
This review highlighted the transformative potential of EHG as 
a non-invasive, reliable, and clinically relevant tool for monitoring 
uterine activity and predicting obstetric outcomes. Advances in signal 
processing and AI/ML-based algorithms have significantly improved 
the accuracy and efficiency of EHG in tasks such as uterine contraction 
detection, term/preterm labor prediction, and labor/non-labor classi-
fication. However, the lack of standardized measurement protocols, 
limited access to large and diverse datasets, and inconsistent evaluation 
methodologies present significant challenges.
To fully realize the potential of EHG, future research should pri-
oritize standardization, the creation of comprehensive datasets that 
include diverse physiological and pathological conditions, and the 
integration of clinical features and heart rate information derived 
from the NI-fECG signals. The development of wearable devices and 
telemedicine solutions could further revolutionize prenatal care, im-
proving accessibility and comfort for pregnant women.
By addressing these challenges, EHG has the potential to become 
an integral part of modern obstetrics, providing critical insights into 
maternal and fetal health while facilitating early detection and inter-
vention in high-risk pregnancies.
